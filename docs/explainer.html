Erasing Weak Activations

Most of what a neural net's neurons do is barely a whisper. Can you just delete the whispers?

We tried it on a small language model (Qwen3-1.7B) and then asked it 14,000 trivia questions about people and places, famous and obscure. Here is what happened.

1Neurons mostly fire weakly

Each of the model's 172,000 neurons fires a little or a lot on every word it reads. Line up one neuron's firings and it looks like this: a few big spikes and a sea of tiny ones. Everything under the dashed line counts as "weak."

2Erase half of all firings: the model shrugs. Erase the same amount at random: it collapses.

We set every weak firing to zero across the whole model while it answered trivia questions. Then we repeated the test, erasing just as many firings but chosen at random instead of by weakness.

Prediction error tripled. The model produces gibberish.

So the weak firings really are close to dead weight. You can throw out more than half of everything the neurons do and the model keeps nearly all of its knowledge.

3Push harder, and the famous facts die first

One theory said the whispers are where the model hides its rarest knowledge, so obscure facts should vanish first. We split the trivia questions into obscure and famous subjects (by Wikipedia page views) and kept erasing, up to 77% of all firings.

At 82% erased it is 76% kept for obscure versus 60% for famous. The gap holds across every cutoff we tried in this range, with confidence intervals that do not include zero.

The obscure facts are, if anything, the sturdier ones. Whatever is in the weak firings, it is not a secret stash of rare knowledge.

The short version

Weak neuron firings carry very little, and what little they carry is not rare facts. The "delete the whispers" side of the argument holds up on a language model.

Caveats: randomly damaging the model also hurts famous facts first, so that ordering looks like a general property of damage rather than something special about weak firings. And the obscure questions start out mostly unanswered (10% correct versus 28% for famous), so some of their surviving answers may be lucky guesses.
